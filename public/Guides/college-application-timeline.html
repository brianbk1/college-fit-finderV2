The College Application Timeline: What to Do Junior and Senior Year, Month by Month

The single most common regret students report about the college process isn't a rejection letter — it's compression. Waiting until senior fall to start means writing essays, requesting recommendations, taking tests, and finalizing a list all at once, on top of the hardest academic semester of high school. Spread the same work across eighteen months and each piece becomes manageable.

Here's a realistic roadmap. Nobody follows a timeline perfectly, and that's fine — the point is knowing what's coming so nothing lands on you as a surprise.

Junior Year: Fall (September–December)

Focus: academics and self-discovery

  • Protect your GPA. Junior year grades are the last full year colleges see when they evaluate your application. This is the year that matters most on your transcript.
  • Take the PSAT in October. Beyond National Merit consideration, it's your first realistic diagnostic for the SAT and tells you where to focus prep.
  • Start noticing what you actually like. Which classes energize you? Which extracurriculars would you do even if nobody was watching? These observations become your major preferences and, eventually, your essays.

Junior Year: Winter (January–March)

Focus: testing and the first draft of your list

  • Take your first SAT or ACT. A spring junior-year test date leaves room for a retake in the fall without deadline pressure. Most students improve on a second sitting.
  • Build a first college list of 15–25 schools. This list is supposed to be too long — you'll cut it later. Cast wide across selectivity levels, sizes, regions, and price points. A tool like College Fit Finder can generate a personalized starting list based on your academic profile, budget, and preferences in a few minutes.
  • Talk finances with your family — now, not next year. The most painful moment in the college process is falling in love with a school in April of senior year and discovering it's unaffordable. An honest budget conversation in junior winter prevents it.
Counselor's tip: When you build your first list, note each school's net price for families like yours using its net price calculator — not the sticker price. Our financial aid guide explains why the difference is enormous.

Junior Year: Spring (April–June)

Focus: visits and recommendations

  • Visit campuses while school is in session. Spring break of junior year is the classic window. Seeing three or four different types of schools (large public, small private, urban, rural) teaches you more about your preferences than seeing ten similar ones. See our campus visit guide for what to actually look for.
  • Ask teachers for recommendations before summer. Teachers get flooded with requests in September. Asking in May — politely, in person — puts you at the front of the line and gives them a relaxed summer to write. Choose teachers who know you, not just teachers who gave you an A.
  • Retake the SAT/ACT if your first score was below your target.

Summer Before Senior Year (June–August)

Focus: essays and list-cutting

  • Write your main personal essay. This is the highest-leverage use of summer. A draft written in July can be revised five times by October; a draft started in October gets revised once, at midnight.
  • Cut your list to 8–12 schools with a deliberate mix: roughly 2–3 reach, 4–5 match, 2–3 safety. Every school on the final list should be one you'd genuinely attend.
  • Open your Common App account in August when the new application year launches, and fill in the biographical sections early.

Senior Year: September–October

Focus: early deadlines and FAFSA

  • Decide on Early Decision or Early Action. ED is binding — only use it for a clear first choice your family has confirmed is affordable. EA is non-binding and generally worth doing everywhere it's offered. Most early deadlines fall November 1 or November 15.
  • File the FAFSA as soon as it opens. Some state and institutional aid is first-come, first-served. If any of your schools require the CSS Profile, file that too.
  • Confirm your recommenders and send them your "brag sheet" — a one-page summary of your activities, interests, and anything you'd love them to mention.
  • Final SAT/ACT sitting if you're still chasing a score, no later than October for early applications.

Senior Year: November–December

Focus: regular decision applications

  • Submit early applications by their deadlines, then keep working on regular decision essays — don't wait for early results.
  • Finish supplemental essays before winter break. "Why this college?" essays reward specificity: name programs, professors, traditions, and opportunities you've actually researched.
  • Early results arrive mid-December. Deferrals are not rejections — send a brief letter of continued interest with any new accomplishments.

Senior Year: January–March

Focus: finishing and waiting well

  • Most regular decision deadlines fall January 1–15. Submit a day or two early; application portals crash on deadline night every single year.
  • Keep your grades up. Admissions offers are conditional on your final transcript, and colleges do rescind offers over dramatic senior-year slides.
  • Watch for financial aid deadlines, which are sometimes different from admissions deadlines.

Senior Year: April–May

Focus: deciding

  • Compare offers on net cost, not prestige. Line up every financial aid letter and compute what your family actually pays per year at each school. Award letters format things differently — sometimes deliberately — so normalize them yourself.
  • Attend admitted-student days at your top two or three choices. Visiting as an admit feels completely different from visiting as an applicant.
  • Deposit by May 1 (National College Decision Day) at exactly one school. Then tell your counselor, thank your recommenders, and exhale.

The One-Sentence Version

Test and explore junior year, write and cut in the summer, apply in the fall, decide on net cost in the spring. Students who follow even a loose version of this rhythm consistently report a calmer, better-decided process than those who compress it into senior fall.
