The Campus Visit Guide: What to Look For and What to Ask

The official campus tour is a marketing product. It's not dishonest — the buildings are real, the tour guide's enthusiasm is usually genuine — but it's curated. The route avoids the tired dorms, the guide is trained to bridge from any question back to a talking point, and the admissions video plays the same swelling music at every school in America. If you want to know what a campus is actually like, you need a plan for the hours around the tour, not just the tour itself.

Before You Go

  • Visit while class is in session. A campus in July tells you about the architecture. A campus in October tells you about the people. If summer is your only option, still go — but weight your impressions accordingly.
  • Register officially. Many colleges track "demonstrated interest," and a registered visit can genuinely help borderline applications at schools that consider it.
  • Book beyond the tour. Ask admissions whether you can sit in on a class in your intended major, meet a professor, or eat in a dining hall. Most schools accommodate these requests — but only if you ask in advance.

During the Tour: Listen for What's Not Said

Take the tour — it's efficient orientation. But listen critically:

  • When you ask about class sizes, does the guide cite the intro courses in your major, or the school-wide average that's flattered by tiny senior seminars?
  • Ask the guide something they can't have rehearsed: "What's something you'd change about this school?" or "What do students complain about?" A thoughtful answer signals a healthy culture; a deflection is information too.
  • Ask how easy it was to get into the classes they wanted. Course registration frustration is one of the most common — and least advertised — student complaints at big universities.

After the Tour: The Unscripted Hour

This is where the real visit happens. Budget at least an hour to wander without a guide:

  • Eat in a dining hall. You'll eat this food two thousand times. More importantly, you'll see students in their natural state — are tables mixed and loud, or is everyone eating alone with headphones?
  • Read the bulletin boards. Flyers are the campus's unfiltered diary: club meetings, protests, concerts, tutoring, roommate searches. A wall crowded with events means things happen here.
  • Pick up the student newspaper. Thirty minutes with the campus paper tells you what students actually care about and what the administration is being criticized for.
  • Visit the buildings for your major — not just the gleaming new engineering center on the tour route. Are labs and studios open and in use? Do department doors have office hours posted and student work displayed?
  • Test the surroundings. Walk (or drive) the area around campus. Could you get groceries, a haircut, a part-time job? For urban campuses, do students seem to use the city or hide from it?
The 9 p.m. test: If your schedule allows, come back to campus in the evening. Libraries, quads, and student centers at night reveal the real balance of a school's social and academic life better than anything the daytime tour shows you.

Questions Worth Asking Real Students

Students not employed by the admissions office are your best source. Most are happy to talk if you're polite. Good openers:

  • "What do you wish you'd known before you chose this school?"
  • "What do people do on a typical Friday night?" (The answer tells you whether the social life matches what you want — whatever that is.)
  • "How accessible are professors, really?"
  • "Do people stay on campus over the weekend, or does it empty out?" (A "suitcase school" is a specific experience — fine if you know it going in.)

Capture Impressions Immediately

After your third campus, they blur — every school has a green quad, a proud library, a legend about a statue. Before you leave the parking lot, spend five minutes recording: three specific things you liked, two concerns, one gut-feeling score out of ten, and any facts you learned that surprised you. Families who do this report that the notes, not memory, ended up driving the final decision months later.

Can't Visit? Do This Instead

Visits are expensive, and nobody can tour twelve schools. For the rest of your list: take the official virtual tour, then go beyond it — watch unofficial "day in my life" videos made by current students, read the student newspaper online, and attend a virtual information session where you can ask live questions. Save in-person visits for after acceptances, when admitted-student days let you compare your genuine finalists with the decision actually at stake.

And before you plan any visit, make sure the school genuinely belongs on your list — the right academic, financial, and campus-culture fit is what makes a visit worth the trip in the first place.
