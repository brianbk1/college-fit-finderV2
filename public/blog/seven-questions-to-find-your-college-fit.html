Seven Questions to Find Your College Fit

Love the campus. Check the fit. A practical guide to looking beyond the tour and imagining your everyday life at college.

The tour guide is welcoming. The campus looks beautiful. You can already picture the sweatshirt. That excitement matters—but before you move a school to the top of your list, picture an ordinary Tuesday: getting to class, finding help, meeting friends and paying for everything.

A college can make a wonderful first impression and still miss something essential for you. Use these seven questions to turn a good campus visit into a better college decision. Bring them to a tour, an admissions conversation or a virtual visit.

1. Can I follow the academic path I want?

Look past the name of the major. Ask about first-year courses, access to your intended program and opportunities to explore another field. If you are undecided, ask how students choose a major and what happens if they change direction. For a structured program, find out whether admission to the university also means admission to that program.

2. What would my family actually pay?

Use each college’s net price calculator for an initial estimate, then ask the financial aid office about your situation. Net price is the cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships; loans help finance a cost rather than remove it. [1] Check housing, meals, transportation and any program fees, plus the conditions for renewing scholarships.

3. How would I get help when I need it?

Ask who your adviser would be, how appointments work and where students turn when a class becomes difficult. College Board recommends asking about academic advising and typical class sizes. [2] Make the question specific: if you struggled in your first month, whom would you contact and how soon could you meet?

4. Where would I find my people?

Think about the social life you want: club sports, music, cultural organizations, volunteering, Greek life or quieter evenings with friends. Ask students what they actually do on weekends and how newcomers join activities. A long list of clubs is a starting point; your goal is to identify a few communities you would genuinely try.

5. Can I manage everyday life without surprises?

Ask about housing after the first year, meal options, campus transportation and car policies. If you expect to work, consider how you would reach a job. If you need accommodations, speak directly with the relevant office. Walk—or virtually trace—the route between likely housing, classrooms and the places you would use regularly.

6. Does the location work beyond the best day of the year?

Consider the weather during the academic year, the journey home and the surrounding community. A school that looks close on a map may require an awkward trip. A city campus and a small-town campus offer different routines. Think about how often you want to go home and what time, travel and cost that would involve.

7. What evidence would change my mind?

Before leaving, identify one concern to investigate and one assumption to test. Ask a current student what surprised them after enrolling. College Board recommends talking with students, exploring facilities and the surrounding area, and taking notes. [3] Look for concrete examples that help you judge your own priorities, while remembering that one student’s experience is only one perspective.

Compare the things that matter to you

After each visit, write down three things you liked, three questions still open and any concern that could rule the school out. Have students and parents do this separately before comparing notes. It makes room for an honest conversation before one person’s first impression becomes everyone’s conclusion.

Separate essentials from preferences. An affordable path to your intended program might be essential. A particular climate or athletic atmosphere might be a strong preference. Decide what belongs in each category before a favorite school tempts you to move the goalposts.
